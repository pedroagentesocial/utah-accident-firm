Almost everyone gets the same call: an adjuster, friendly and fast, with a number. The number sounds like a decision. It is not — it is an opening position, and it is built to be accepted quickly.

## How the number gets built

Adjusters work from software and comparable listings, not from what your car means to you. The output depends entirely on what goes in:

- **The comparables chosen.** Which local listings, in what condition, at what mileage.
- **Condition assumptions.** Software often assumes "average" unless someone proves otherwise.
- **Deductions** for wear, prior damage or mileage, which are negotiable when the assumption is wrong.
- **Only the visible damage.** Anything hidden behind the bumper is not in the first estimate.

## What the first offer usually leaves out

- **Supplements.** The extra damage the shop finds after teardown. This is normal and routine — but it means the first estimate was never the real cost.
- **Storage and towing** already accruing at the yard.
- **The rental**, or loss of use if you are without a car and no rental was authorized.
- **Diminished value** — that a repaired car can be worth less than an equivalent one that was never hit.
- **Recent work**: new tires, a new battery, service records. All of it is evidence, and none of it counts unless somebody submits it.

## What actually moves the number

Not arguing. Evidence:

1. **Ask for the valuation report** that produced the offer. You are entitled to see how they got there.
2. **Check the comparables** against real local listings for the same trim, mileage and condition.
3. **Submit your documentation**: photos, service history, receipts for recent work.
4. **Get a second estimate** from a shop you choose. You are not required to use the insurer's network.
5. **Put disagreements in writing**, with numbers attached.

## When it stalls

Claims rarely get denied outright; they go quiet. Days pass, the adjuster is "reviewing", the shop is waiting for authorization, and nobody calls anybody.
